The most common question I get isn't about programming. It's a number. "How many days a week should I be training?" People want a single answer, and the honest one is a range: three to six, and where you land depends on your experience, your goal, and how much life you can give to this. Here's the actual breakdown instead of the range.

## The short answer by experience level

**New to one or both sports: 3-4 days.** If you're a lifter who just started running, or a runner who just picked up a barbell, three days is enough and four is plenty. You're not limited by willpower — you're limited by tissue. Your tendons, your shins, the connective tissue around your joints adapt far slower than your enthusiasm does. More days early on doesn't build more fitness; it builds injuries. Three sessions done consistently for six months beats five sessions done for three weeks before your shins force a stop.

**Intermediate, a year or two in both: 4-5 days.** Once you've got a base in both and your tissues have caught up, four to five days is the sweet spot for most hybrid athletes. It's enough to hold real strength and build genuine running fitness at the same time. Four days is two lifts and two runs; five adds a third run, usually an easy one. Most people reading this should be here.

**Advanced, training seriously for years: 5-6 days.** Six days is what I do — two strength sessions and four runs — and it's the ceiling for almost anyone with a job and a family. Beyond six days you're not adding fitness, you're subtracting recovery. The seventh day isn't a training day; it's the day that makes the other six work.

Notice the number doesn't climb forever. There's no version of this where ten sessions a week is better. Past a point, every session you add is a session's worth of recovery you take away from the ones that matter.

## It's really a question about your goal

Experience sets the floor. Your goal sets where in the range you land.

**Maintain strength, build running.** This is the most common hybrid goal, and it needs the fewest days. Two lifts a week holds your strength — I held a 170 kg deadlift for months on two sessions while running 50+ km. Everything else is running. So a maintain-strength goal fits comfortably in four days: two lifts, two runs, and you can push to five if you want more mileage.

**Build both at once.** Ambitious, and it works, but it's the most expensive goal in days. You need at least three quality stimuli — two lifts and one hard run, or two runs and one hard lift — plus easy volume around them. That's five days minimum, realistically six. And even then, progress in both is slower than it'd be in either alone. That's the tax on being hybrid. It's worth paying; just don't expect single-sport progress rates.

**Peak for an event.** During a marathon build or a race prep, running days go up and lifting drops to two maintenance sessions. Total days might stay at five or six, but the mix shifts hard toward the sport you're peaking for. [Training for a marathon without losing your squat](/blog/marathon-training-without-losing-strength) is exactly this shift, and [the taper](/blog/marathon-taper-without-losing-strength) is where days come back down.

## The tradeoffs nobody tells you

More days is not more progress past a point, and the failure mode is specific. Here's what actually goes wrong when people add days.

**Every added day is a recovery day removed.** Fatigue is one budget shared between lifting and running. A hybrid athlete accumulates it faster than a single-sport athlete because both systems are being stressed at once. Add a sixth session and you haven't added a sixth session's worth of adaptation — you've spread the same recovery capacity thinner across more work.

**The junk-day trap.** The most common mistake I see is a five- or six-day week where three of the sessions are moderate — a not-quite-easy run, a not-quite-hard lift. Moderate everything is the worst place to live: tiring enough to cost recovery, not hard enough to drive adaptation. Fewer, more polarized sessions — genuinely easy easy days, genuinely hard hard days — beat more moderate ones. [Keeping easy runs actually easy](/blog/zone-2-running-for-lifters) is what lets you fit more days without the junk piling up.

**Doubles are a day-count multiplier, and a trap.** If you're trying to fit six sessions into four days, you're doubling up — and three of those doubles will be compromised. [Running and lifting on the same day](/blog/running-and-lifting-same-day) works within rules, but stacking sessions to hit a session count is usually a sign the week has too many sessions, not too few days.

So the honest rule: pick the fewest days that meet your goal, not the most days you can survive. Three consistent days beat five ragged ones every single time.

## FAQ

### Is 3 days a week enough for a hybrid athlete?

Yes, especially if you're new to one of the sports or your goal is to maintain strength while building running. Three well-chosen sessions — one lift, one hard run, one long run, or two lifts and one run — done consistently beat five inconsistent ones. Add days as your tissues adapt, not before.

### Can you train 6 days a week doing both lifting and running?

Yes, and it's the realistic ceiling for a serious hybrid athlete: two strength sessions and four runs, with one full rest day. Beyond six days you subtract recovery faster than you add fitness. The rest day is what makes the other six productive.

### How many days should I lift versus run?

For most hybrid goals, two lifts a week maintains strength, and the rest of your days go to running. If strength is the priority, three lifts and two runs. If an event is the priority, two maintenance lifts and everything else running. Two lifting sessions is the floor that keeps your numbers.
