Every lifter who signs up for their first Hyrox asks the same question, usually in a roundabout way: is this going to cost me my squat?

It's a fair question. You've spent years building strength you don't want to hand back. And the honest answer is yes — a little. But "a little" and "a lot" are not decided by Hyrox. They're decided by how you build the eight weeks in front of it.

Most people get this wrong in one of two directions. They keep their full lifting split and bolt running on top, then wonder why both stall. Or they panic, drop the barbell, do CrossFit-style conditioning five times a week, and arrive on race day having quietly detrained the strength the stations are there to test. There's a version in the middle that keeps most of your lifting *and* gets you across eight kilometers of running with a sled in the way. This is how to build it.

## First, the honest cost

The interference effect is real. Training strength and endurance at the same time produces smaller strength gains than training strength alone — that's been the finding since Hickson first documented it in 1980, and the meta-analyses since (Wilson et al., 2012) have mostly argued about the size of the effect, not whether it exists.

Here's what the size actually is, and why it's less scary than gym culture says:

- Max strength takes a hit of roughly **5–10%** when you add endurance work — reduced, not erased.
- **Hypertrophy is barely affected.** You keep the muscle; it's peak force production that dips first (Murach & Bagley, 2016).
- **Running interferes more than cycling**, because of the eccentric pounding, and **volume is the lever** — 3–4 runs a week is manageable, 60km+ is where it bites.
- **Squat suffers more than deadlift.** You're squatting on chronically tired legs; the hip-dominant deadlift recovers faster. A squat that drops more than your deadlift isn't a training error, it's the normal pattern.

I wrote up the full picture, with my own lift numbers across a marathon block, in [does running kill your gains](/blog/interference-effect-does-running-kill-gains). The short version for Hyrox: you can hold most of your strength on **two heavy sessions a week** if the plan protects them. The gap between losing 5% and losing 25% is entirely spacing and volume.

For the record, mine: squat 210 kg before I ran seriously, 150 kg after; deadlift 300 kg to about 200 kg. That's a bigger drop than 5–10%, and I know exactly why — I chose the marathon, I let the lifting go to zero for stretches, and I was running 70 km weeks at 90 kg body weight on five hours of sleep. On two heavy sessions a week with the running capped, I've since held a 170 kg deadlift for months without trying very hard. The honest version of the cost is in [the origin post](/blog/from-300kg-deadlift-to-340-marathon).

## The two rules that keep your lifts

Before any weekly grid, two decisions do most of the work.

**Rule 1: Don't let the hard sessions collide.** Same-session concurrent training — a lift and a hard run back to back on the same tired legs — is the worst case for interference. Heavy squats the day before your interval run is nearly as bad. Separate hard efforts by time (a morning lift and an evening run is ideal) or by days. Easy runs can go anywhere; they barely register.

**Rule 2: Two heavy compound sessions, kept at maintenance.** You cannot run 40km a week *and* run a five-day powerlifting split. Something gives. So cut to two strength sessions, keep one heavy compound in each (a squat pattern and a deadlift or press pattern), work in the 2–3 hard-set range, and drop most accessories. I've held a heavy deadlift for months on two sessions a week. Maintenance is a real training goal, not a consolation prize.

Get those two right and the rest of the plan is just filling in the aerobic base and the station-specific work.

## What the race is actually asking your gym work to do

Hyrox is eight 1km runs with a functional station between each — over 8km of running total, wrapped around strength work, usually an hour-plus of continuous effort. Nothing on the station list needs a big one-rep max. A 140kg squatter already has all the absolute strength the sled needs. What it needs is *repeatable* force while your heart rate is pinned from running.

So a lifter's gym training doesn't need to get stronger for Hyrox. It needs to get more repeatable. Here's the translation:

| Station | What it tests | What to train for it |
|---------|---------------|----------------------|
| Sled Push / Pull | Leg drive, repeated | Heavy sled if you have it; otherwise hill sprints, leg-press or belt-squat intervals, walking lunges |
| Farmers Carry | Grip, trunk, walking economy | Loaded carries for distance, not load PRs; heavy holds |
| Sandbag Lunges | Single-leg strength endurance | Walking and reverse lunges, step-ups, 15–25 reps |
| Wall Balls | Legs + shoulders when both are cooked | Wall balls or thrusters in sets of 20+; front squats for the pattern |
| SkiErg / Row | Upper and full-body aerobic endurance | Erg intervals; keep some horizontal pulling in the strength work |

Notice the rep ranges. Your gym work for Hyrox lives in the 15–25 range for the station patterns, on top of the two heavy compounds that keep your base. Fives and triples build a squat; they don't build the legs that survive round six.

## Compromised running: the skill lifters skip

Here's the part that humbles strong people. Your 1km pace after a 50m sled push is nowhere near your fresh 1km pace, and the gap between them is where the race is decided. Coaches call it **compromised running** — running on legs that are already trashed. You can be a genuinely fit runner and still blow up in round three because you never rehearsed the transition.

Once a week, put running and station work in the same session on purpose: run 800m–1km at target pace, hit 15–20 wall balls or a sled push, repeat for 4–6 rounds. The goal is calibration, not destruction. You're teaching your legs what race pace feels like *after* a station, so on race day nothing surprises them.

## The 8-week plan

Eight weeks is enough to build the aerobic base a lifter is usually missing and sharpen the race-specific work, without so long that your strength drifts. It breaks into four blocks.

| Block | Weeks | Running | Strength | Hyrox-specific |
|-------|-------|---------|----------|----------------|
| **Base** | 1–2 | Build easy volume; one short tempo | Heavy — squat, deadlift, press near normal, 3–5 sets | Learn the movements: sled, wall balls, erg technique |
| **Build** | 3–5 | Add one interval session; volume peaks | Two sessions: one heavy compound at maintenance + one strength-endurance | One compromised-running session per week |
| **Specific** | 6–7 | Race-pace running; hold volume | Maintenance only — keep the heavy single, drop accessories | Race simulation every ~10 days at target pace |
| **Taper** | 8 | Cut volume 40–50%; keep intensity | Same weight, fewer reps; last session ~5 days out | One short sharpener, then rest |

The through-line: strength volume steps down exactly as race-specific work steps up, so at no point are both maxed at once. That's the whole interference game — sequence the hard things so they take turns instead of fighting.

### A week from the Build block

This is where a lifter's plan usually falls apart, so here's a full seven days done right:

| Day | Session | Why it's here |
|-----|---------|---------------|
| Mon | Heavy strength — squat + deadlift pattern, 3 working sets each, plus carries | Freshest day of the week; the session that protects your strength base |
| Tue | Easy run, 8–10km, conversational | Recovery pace on legs still tired from Monday — nothing hard stacked on heavy lifting |
| Wed | Compromised running: 5× (800m at race pace + 20 wall balls / 20m sled) | Sits *between* the two strength days, never the day after leg day |
| Thu | Strength endurance — lunges, step-ups, ski or row intervals, RPE 7 | A different pattern from Monday; builds station endurance without max load |
| Fri | Rest, or 30 min easy spin | Protects Saturday's long run |
| Sat | Long easy run, 12–16km | The aerobic base the race actually runs on — the highest-return session in the week |
| Sun | Rest | The adaptation happens here, not in the gym |

Two things to see. The week has far more easy aerobic running than station work — that's deliberate, because Hyrox is mostly an aerobic race and easy volume is the limiter for anyone coming from lifting. And the two hard leg days (Monday's heavy strength, Wednesday's compromised running) are spaced so they don't poison each other. The order matters as much as the content.

**Easy runs must be easy.** This is the single hardest habit for lifters to build. An easy run that creeps into a moderate grind because you're too proud to run slowly is the exact recovery debt that eats your Monday squats. Keep the easy days genuinely easy so the hard days can be genuinely hard.

## The taper

The last block is a taper, and the logic is the same as a marathon taper, just compressed: **cut volume, keep intensity, arrive fresh.** Drop running volume 40–50% over the final week or so, keep some race-pace touches, and for strength, hold the weight but cut the reps — the same-weight-fewer-reps approach that maintains the neuromuscular signal without the fatigue cost. Your last real strength session lands about five days out. The full breakdown, with exact numbers, is in [how to taper without losing strength](/blog/marathon-taper-without-losing-strength).

## The mistakes lifters make specifically

1. **Treating the sled like a one-rep max.** It's a repeatable submaximal effort under fatigue, not a max attempt. Redline the first sled on adrenaline and rounds five through eight become a death march.
2. **Skipping the easy runs because they feel pointless.** They're the engine. If most of your training is stations and hard efforts, your race is decided by round four. The boring runs are the ones that win it.
3. **Stacking the run and the lift on the same tired legs.** Same-session concurrent work is the worst case for interference. Separate hard efforts by six hours or by days.
4. **Keeping powerlifting volume.** You can't run this much and run a five-day split. Cut to two heavy sessions, drop the accessories, and trust that maintenance holds more than you'd expect.
5. **Under-eating.** Running burns real calories. A lifter drifting into a deficit — on purpose or by accident — loses strength faster than the interference effect alone ever would. Eat at maintenance or above.

Number 2 was mine. In my first marathon block I ran too many "easy" runs at a medium effort because slow felt wrong coming from powerlifting, and I paid for it on race day — the last 12 km were survival. The boring runs are the ones that win it.

## FAQ

### How many weeks do I need to train for Hyrox as a lifter?

Eight is enough if you can already run 5 km without stopping and have a strength base. It's long enough to build the easy aerobic volume you're missing and to rehearse compromised running, and short enough that two heavy sessions a week hold your lifts. With no running base at all, add four weeks of easy running in front of it.

### Will I lose my squat training for Hyrox?

Some, not much. Expect a 5–10% dip on two heavy sessions a week with the running capped, and less than that if you keep the load heavy and cut volume rather than intensity. The squat drops more than the deadlift because it shares the most tissue with the running.

### How many days a week should I train for Hyrox?

Five in the build block: two strength sessions, one compromised-running session, one easy run and one long run. Four works if you fold the easy run into a strength day. Six is for people with a base in both and the recovery to match.
