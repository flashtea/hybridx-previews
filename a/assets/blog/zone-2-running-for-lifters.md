The first few weeks I added running to my lifting, I ran every run like it was a test. Finish gasping, legs full, and then Monday's squats felt like garbage. I decided running was killing my strength. It wasn't the running. It was the pace.

Zone 2 is the fix, and it's the part lifters get wrong most often, because running slowly on purpose feels like doing nothing. Here's what it is, how to do it, and why it's the one kind of running that doesn't fight your lifts.

## What zone 2 actually is

Zone 2 is easy aerobic running. The textbook definition is 60-70% of your maximum heart rate. The definition that works when you're out on the road is the talk test: you can hold a full conversation, complete sentences, not gasped words. If you can only manage three words at a time, you're too fast. If you could sing, you're too slow — but almost nobody errs on the too-slow side.

For a lifter, the honest warning is this: **zone 2 will feel insultingly slow at first.** Coming from the gym, where every working set is meant to be hard, a pace you can chat through feels like cheating. Plenty of lifters new to running can't hold zone 2 without walking the hills. That's normal. It's not a sign you're unfit — it's a sign your aerobic base hasn't been built yet, because you've spent years building everything else.

The pace fixes itself. Within 8-12 weeks of consistent easy running, your heart rate at the same pace drops noticeably. The 6:30/km that pinned you at 150 bpm becomes 6:00/km at the same heart rate. You didn't get tougher. You built the aerobic engine that lets you run faster while staying easy.

## Why it doesn't cost you strength

This is the part lifters care about, so here are the actual numbers. The [interference effect](/blog/interference-effect-does-running-kill-gains) — the reason cardio supposedly kills gains — is real but overstated: strength drops about 5-10% under real endurance volume, and muscle size barely moves. And the interference scales with how hard and how much you run.

Zone 2 is the least costly running there is. It's low intensity, so it doesn't dig into the same recovery budget your heavy squats need. Hard intervals and tempo runs are what tax your legs and blunt the next lift; an easy 45-minute jog does not. This is why the bulk of a hybrid athlete's running — 70-80% of it — should be zone 2. You spend most of your running time cheap, so you can afford to lift heavy and run one genuinely hard session a week.

Two rules keep it cheap:

1. **Keep easy days easy.** The trap is turning a zone 2 run into a moderate one because you felt good. Moderate-intensity running is the worst of both worlds — hard enough to fatigue your legs, not hard enough to drive real adaptation. [The hardest rule in hybrid training](/blog/from-300kg-deadlift-to-340-marathon) is running slow when you're able to run fast.
2. **Run flat and soft where you can.** Zone 2 on a hilly route becomes zone 3 on every climb. If you're using easy runs to protect your lifts, keep the route flat, and give the eccentric-heavy stuff — long downhills — its own budget.

## How slow, how long, how often

Concrete guidance, the way I'd give it to a lifter starting out.

**How slow.** Whatever pace keeps you in full sentences. For most lifters that's 60-90 seconds per kilometer slower than they think they should run. Check it against heart rate if you have a strap, but trust the talk test over the watch — chest straps drift, and wrist optical readings on a cold morning lie by 20 bpm.

**How long.** Start at 20-30 minutes. That's enough to build without wrecking your legs for the next session. Grow the longest easy run by about 10% a week — 30 minutes becomes 33, not 45. Your heart adapts in weeks; your tendons and the connective tissue around a heavier body take months. If you weigh over 85 kg, the impact math isn't on your side early on, so ramp slower than a lighter runner would.

**How often.** Two to three easy runs a week is plenty alongside two lifts. That's a maintenance-to-building dose of aerobic work that leaves your strength intact. If you only have room for one run, make it the long easy one — it's the single most valuable run a lifter can do.

A simple week: an easy 30-40 minute run twice, and one longer easy run of 50-70 minutes on the weekend. No intervals at all for the first two months. Get the base in first, then add one quality session. [How many days a week you should train](/blog/how-many-days-a-week-hybrid-athlete) sorts out how these runs fit around your lifts.

Where does zone 2 sit relative to leg day? Not the day after a hard session, ideally — but an easy run the day after heavy squats is actually useful. It flushes the legs without adding real fatigue. The [same-day and next-day sequencing rules](/blog/running-and-lifting-same-day) cover the spacing in detail.

I put most of my own running in zone 2 and let [HybridX](/) write the paces, because the discipline of keeping easy runs easy is the thing I quietly break when I'm programming by feel. When the plan tells me the target is 6:00/km and heart rate under 145, I run that instead of racing my own shadow.

## When to add the hard stuff

Once you can run 40 minutes easy without your heart rate climbing out of zone 2, you've earned one quality session a week — intervals or a tempo. One. The zone 2 work stays the foundation; the hard run is the sharpening on top. Add a second hard session only when you're training for something specific and have dropped a lifting day to make room.

And build recovery in. Every fourth week or so, both the running and the lifting need a back-off at the same time — [the hybrid deload](/blog/deload-and-recovery-hybrid-week) is how you take one without losing the base you built.

## FAQ

### How slow should zone 2 running be for a lifter?

Slow enough to hold a full conversation — usually 60-90 seconds per kilometer slower than feels natural at first. Use the talk test over your watch. It will feel too easy; that's the point. The pace speeds up on its own after 8-12 weeks of consistent easy running.

### Will zone 2 running make me lose muscle or strength?

No, not at the doses a hybrid athlete runs. Zone 2 is low intensity and doesn't compete hard with strength recovery. Strength drops 5-10% only under high running volume, and muscle size barely changes. Keep two lifts a week with the big compounds and you'll hold both.

### How many zone 2 runs per week should I do?

Two to three alongside two lifting sessions is a good starting point. If you only have room for one run, make it a longer easy one. Keep 70-80% of your total running in zone 2 and save the hard efforts for one quality session a week.
