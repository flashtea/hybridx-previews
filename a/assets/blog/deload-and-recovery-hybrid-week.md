Around week six of any block, everything starts to feel heavy. The warm-up sets that used to fly up drag. The easy runs creep slower at the same heart rate. Nothing's injured, nothing's wrong — you're just accumulating fatigue faster than you're clearing it. That's the signal for a deload, and hybrid athletes hit it sooner than single-sport athletes because they're taxing two systems at once.

Here's when to take one, what a hybrid deload actually looks like, and how to space a hard run and a heavy lift when you're not deloading so you don't have to bail early.

## When to back off — both at once

The mistake is deloading one sport and grinding the other. If your legs are cooked, cutting running volume while keeping heavy squats doesn't rescue the week — the fatigue is shared, so the back-off has to be shared too. When you deload, deload both.

Two ways to time it.

**On a schedule.** Most hybrid athletes do well on a three-weeks-build, one-week-deload cycle. If you're training three to five days a week and not peaking for anything, you can stretch that to a deload every 6-8 weeks. The two-system load means I'd err toward the shorter end — every fourth week — rather than pushing to eight and finding out the hard way.

**On symptoms.** The schedule is a default; your body overrides it. Take a deload early if your resting heart rate is up several beats for a few days, your easy pace is slower at the same effort, your working weights feel heavier than the number says, your sleep is off, or the motivation to train just isn't there. One of these is noise. Three at once is your body asking for the back-off.

A deload is not a rest week. You still train — you just cut the work. The point is to let adaptation catch up to the training you've already done, not to lose fitness sitting on the couch. A week of complete rest actually feels worse: you stiffen up, the first session back is flat, and you've lost a little of the aerobic base for no gain. Keep moving, keep it light, and don't use the deload as a window to add new exercises or chase a technique cue — a deload is for recovering the work you've done, not starting new work.

## What a hybrid deload looks like

Concrete numbers, because "take it easy" is useless advice.

**Lifting.** Cut volume by about 40-50% — roughly halve your sets. Keep the intensity moderate: work up to a comfortable RPE 6-7 on the main lifts, no top sets, no grinders. A week that was squat 4×5 becomes 2×5 at a slightly lighter load. You keep the movement pattern and the neural feel; you drop the fatigue.

**Running.** Cut total volume 30-40%, and cut the intensity harder than that. This is the week to drop the intervals entirely and run easy. A week of 40 km with one hard session becomes about 25 km, all in [zone 2](/blog/zone-2-running-for-lifters). Keep the long run but trim it by a third — 90 minutes becomes 60.

**Timing.** Take the deload week when life is already busy if you can — a travel week, a heavy work stretch. It's the one week where missing a session costs you nothing, so spend it where the disruption would have cost you most otherwise.

One distinction worth making: a deload is not a taper. A taper before a race keeps intensity high while dropping volume, so you arrive fresh and sharp. A deload drops both, to recover mid-block. If you're prepping for an event, [the marathon taper](/blog/marathon-taper-without-losing-strength) is a different tool with a different shape — don't confuse the two.

## Sequencing a hard run and a heavy lift 24-48 hours apart

Most of the time you're not deloading, and the way you avoid needing an early one is spacing your hard days properly. The single rule that prevents most accumulated fatigue: **a hard run and a heavy lower-body lift need 24-48 hours between them, not the same day.**

Here's the tissue argument. A heavy squat or deadlift session and a hard interval session both draw on the same legs, and the recovery for each runs about 48 hours for most people. Stack them closer and neither fully recovers before the next hard hit — you're always training the same tired legs, and that's the slow grind that lands you in an early deload.

The spacing rules I use, across days:

- **Never heavy lower-body the day before a hard or long run.** The run gets tired, form-compromised legs, which is where shins and knees complain.
- **Never hard intervals the day after deadlifts.** The reverse of the same problem.
- **48 hours between the two hardest sessions of the week.** If leg day is Monday, the hard run is Wednesday or Thursday, not Tuesday.
- **Easy running is exempt.** An easy zone 2 run the day after squats is fine — even useful, it flushes the legs. It's the *hard* efforts that need the 48-hour gap.

If two hard sessions genuinely have to sit only 24 hours apart because of your schedule, put the one you care about second, on the fresher-ish legs, and cap the first. Better still, if they land on the same day, [the same-day rules](/blog/running-and-lifting-same-day) — six hours apart, easy session in the evening — are how you survive it. And [laying out leg day and running across the week](/blog/leg-day-and-running-same-week) is the fuller version of getting this spacing right.

How many hard days you can fit depends on how many days you train at all — [that math is here](/blog/how-many-days-a-week-hybrid-athlete). Fewer days means fewer hard sessions, which means the spacing mostly takes care of itself.

## FAQ

### How often should a hybrid athlete deload?

Every fourth week is a safe default — three weeks building, one week backing off. If you train three to five days a week and aren't peaking for an event, every 6-8 weeks can be enough. Because hybrid training taxes two systems, err toward the shorter interval, and take one early if the symptoms show up first.

### Should I deload running and lifting at the same time?

Yes. Fatigue is a shared budget, so backing off one while grinding the other doesn't work. Cut lifting volume 40-50% at moderate intensity, cut running volume 30-40% and drop the hard sessions to easy. Deload both, in the same week.

### How many hours between a hard run and a heavy lift?

If they're on separate days, aim for 48 hours between the two hardest sessions. Easy runs don't count — an easy run the day after squats is fine. If a hard run and a heavy lower-body lift must share a day, put six hours between them and make one of the two easy.
