## What you'll lose (and what you'll keep)

Here are my actual numbers. Before running seriously vs now, deep in marathon prep:

| Lift | Powerlifting peak | During marathon prep |
|------|------------------|---------------------|
| Deadlift | 300kg | 170kg x5 (conventional) |
| Squat | 210kg | 100kg x4 |
| Bench | 150kg | ~100kg x5 |

That looks rough on paper. But context matters:

- I switched deadlift from sumo to conventional because sumo + running volume wrecked my knees
- Squat always takes the biggest hit because your legs are never fully fresh
- Bench held up the best with minimal direct work — upper body doesn't compete for the same recovery
- I'm also 15-20kg lighter than my powerlifting days

On the running side: 1:39 half marathon, 3:40 first marathon (with a bad blowup at 30km), and currently tracking for sub-3:30 in my second.

Is that elite at either? No. But it's genuinely strong AND genuinely fast, and that combination is rare.

## The first 8 weeks are the hardest

Your cardiovascular system adapts in weeks. Your joints need months. This gap is where most lifters get hurt or quit.

I started running and within a few weeks my heart and lungs were fine with 30-minute easy runs. But my shins, feet, and ankles were angry from the first step. Coming from powerlifting, where you load heavy and recover, the repetitive impact of running is a completely different stress.

What worked: starting with 2 easy runs per week, genuinely easy. Like embarrassingly slow. If you're coming from strength sports where every set should feel hard, deliberately running at a pace where you could hold a conversation feels wrong. Do it anyway.

## How i structure my training now

This has been stable for months and it works:

- **Strength**: 2 sessions per week. Alternating squat and deadlift focus, one heavy press. Big compounds only, get in and out. Plus daily push-ups and pull-ups for upper body volume.
- **Running**: 4 sessions per week. 2 easy runs, 1 tempo or interval session, 1 long run.
- **Weekly volume**: 45-65km running depending on phase, peaking around 62km during marathon build.

That's 6 sessions in a week. With a full-time job and a baby, every session starts at 6 AM.

## The sequencing problem

This is the thing that nobody warns you about and it matters more than anything else.

Heavy squats tank your running for 48-72 hours. A hard long run tanks your deadlift recovery. If you stack them wrong, you spend the whole week recovering instead of training.

My rules:
- Never heavy lower body the day before a long run
- Never intervals the day after deadlifts
- Easy runs can go anywhere — they're low enough stress
- If something has to give, it's always the strength session, never the run (during marathon prep; reverse this if strength is the priority)

## What to expect, month by month

**Month 1-2**: Your running feels terrible and everything below your knees hurts. Your lifts are fine because running volume is too low to interfere. You'll wonder why anyone does this.

**Month 3-4**: Running starts to click. Easy runs actually feel easy. But your squat starts dropping because accumulated fatigue is building. This is the frustrating phase — you're not yet good at running but you're already losing strength.

**Month 5-6**: Things settle. You find your weekly structure, your body adapts to the dual stress, and you stop comparing your lifts to your powerlifting peak. This is where it starts being fun.

**Month 7+**: You're a hybrid athlete. You stop thinking in terms of "powerlifter who runs" and start thinking in terms of balancing two modalities. The strength losses level off. The running improvements keep coming.

## The actual answer

Can you powerlift and run a marathon? Yes. But you're not powerlifting anymore — you're doing hybrid training. The sooner you accept that framing, the less frustrated you'll be watching your squat drop.

Two strength sessions per week maintains more than you'd expect. Your deadlift holds better than your squat. Your bench barely moves. And you gain something most pure lifters don't have: a cardiovascular system that actually works.
