Four days is the number most people can actually keep. Not the number they'd like — the number that survives a job, a family, and a body that's already tired from both. I train six days myself (two strength sessions, four runs), but almost every message I get from lifters who've started running comes with the same constraint: "I've got four days. What do I do with them?"

Here's the week I'd hand them. It's built around one idea: **two lifts, one hard run, one long run.** Everything else is a compromise you make on purpose.

## What a 4-day hybrid week has to do

With four sessions there is no room for dedicated easy-run days. That's the first thing to accept. The easy running — the stuff that builds the aerobic base — gets squeezed into the long run and, if you want it, a short jog tacked onto a lift. So the week has four jobs:

1. **One lower-body strength session** that keeps the squat pattern moving.
2. **One upper-body session with a hinge** — press, pull, and the deadlift.
3. **One quality run** — the session that makes you faster.
4. **One long run** — the session that makes you a runner at all.

That's it. No arm day. No "conditioning" day. If you're training four days and one of them is an arm day, you're a lifter who runs a bit, and that's fine — but it's a different plan.

## The template week

| Day | Session | Time |
|-----|---------|------|
| Mon | Lower strength — squat 4×5 at RPE 7, Romanian deadlift 3×6 at RPE 7, split squat 3×8 each leg, 3 sets of core | 55–65 min |
| Tue | Quality run — 10 min easy, 5×3 min at threshold with 2 min jog, 10 min easy | 45 min |
| Wed | Off | — |
| Thu | Upper strength + hinge — deadlift 3×3 at RPE 8, bench 4×5 at RPE 7, pull-ups 4 sets, row 3×8 | 55–65 min |
| Fri | Off | — |
| Sat | Long run — 60–90 min in zone 2, conversational the whole way | 60–90 min |
| Sun | Off | — |

A few definitions, because they matter more than the grid:

**RPE 7** means three reps left in the tank at the end of the set. For most people that's 72–78% of a one-rep max on fives, 80–85% on triples. Nothing in this week is a top set. That's deliberate.

**Threshold** is the pace you could hold for about an hour in a race — roughly 10k pace for most people running 20–40 km a week. It should feel like RPE 8 out of 10: hard, controlled, and you can finish every rep at the same pace as the first. If you're fading on rep four, you started too fast.

**Zone 2** is conversational. Full sentences, not gasped words. If you track heart rate, it lands somewhere between 60 and 70% of your max for most people, but the talk test is more honest than the watch. Coming from lifting, this pace will feel like cheating. Run it anyway. [Easy runs must be easy](/blog/from-300kg-deadlift-to-340-marathon) is the hardest rule in the whole plan, and it's the one that decides whether Monday's squats happen.

## Why the days sit where they sit

Look at Monday and Tuesday. A squat session the day before a hard run is the pairing every article tells you to avoid, and with six or seven sessions a week you'd avoid it. With four days you can't put 48 hours between everything, so you choose which collision to accept.

This one is acceptable because Monday is fives at RPE 7, not a top set. Three reps in reserve recovers inside 48 hours for most people; a grinding 5×5 at RPE 9 doesn't. Tuesday's intervals get legs that are tired but working. If you flip it — intervals Monday, heavy squats Tuesday — the squat gets the fatigued day, and lifting tired is less costly than running hard tired: you can lower the bar, whereas running hard on dead legs is where form falls apart and shins complain.

The deadlift lives on Thursday because it's 48 hours from both hard runs. Deadlifts the day before a long run is the one pairing I'd never accept — I learned that the hard way during marathon prep, and the [sequencing rules I ended up with](/blog/can-you-powerlift-and-run) are simple: never heavy lower body the day before a long run, never intervals the day after deadlifts.

If strength is your priority rather than running, swap the shape: quality run Monday, lower strength Wednesday with a top set allowed, upper and hinge Friday, long run Sunday. Same four sessions, and now the squat gets the freshest legs of the week. The [3-, 4- and 5-day schedules](/blog/hybrid-athlete-weekly-schedule-3-4-5-days) post walks through both versions.

## How to progress it

**Strength:** add 2.5 kg to the main lift when every set of the previous week landed at RPE 7 or lower. If a set hits RPE 9, hold the weight. The bar goes up maybe twice a month. That's the pace.

**Running:** add no more than 10% to the week's total minutes, and put the increase into the long run first. 60 minutes becomes 65, then 70. Not 60 to 80 because you felt good on Saturday. Every fourth week, cut the long run back by about 30% and drop the intervals to 3×3 min. Your tendons are on a slower schedule than your lungs — [my shins took months to catch up](/blog/from-300kg-deadlift-to-340-marathon) with a heart that was ready for more.

**When both feel stale:** after 8–12 weeks, change the quality session (3×8 min at half-marathon effort, or 8×400 m faster than 5k pace) and the rep scheme (triples on the squat, fives on the deadlift). Same skeleton, different stimulus.

## What you're giving up

Four days gets you roughly 25–35 km of running and two lifts. That is a maintenance dose of strength and a modest, steady running build. It is not a marathon PR block and it's not a powerlifting peak.

Concretely: two lifting days is enough to hold your numbers. I held a 170 kg deadlift for months on two sessions a week while running 50+ km, and [the research on the interference effect](/blog/interference-effect-does-running-kill-gains) agrees — strength drops 5–10% under real endurance volume, muscle barely changes. Four days won't make you bigger. It will keep you strong while you learn to run.

On the running side, 30 km a week at mostly easy pace, with one quality session, is enough to take a lifter from "can't run 5 km" to a comfortable 10k in a few months, and to a half marathon in about a year. It's not enough for a fast marathon. When you want that, you need a fifth and sixth day, and the plan changes shape — [how to train for a marathon without losing your squat and deadlift](/blog/marathon-training-without-losing-strength) is what that looks like.

## FAQ

### Can you build muscle and run on a 4-day hybrid plan?

Some, if you're new to lifting. If you've been training for years, expect maintenance: two sessions a week with the big compounds at RPE 7–8 holds strength and muscle, it doesn't add much. Eat at maintenance or slightly above — a deficit costs you more strength than the running does.

### Should the quality run or the lower-body lift come first in the week?

Put whichever one matters more on the fresher legs. Running first, the squat gets capped at RPE 7 the day before. Strength first, the intervals move to Monday and the squat gets Wednesday with a top set. Either works; doing both hard on back-to-back days without capping one is what doesn't.

### How long should the long run be on 4 days a week?

Start at 60 minutes if you can already run 30 without stopping, and grow it about 5 minutes a week to 90. Beyond 90 minutes you need more easy volume during the week to support it, which means a fifth day.
