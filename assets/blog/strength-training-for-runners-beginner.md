Most runners start lifting because something hurt. A knee, an IT band, a calf that keeps tearing in the same spot. The strength work gets prescribed by a physio, done for six weeks, and dropped the moment the pain goes. That's the wrong reason and the wrong dose. Two proper sessions a week, kept for a year, does more for your running than almost anything else you can do in the gym — and it won't turn you into a slow, heavy lifter unless you go looking for that.

I came at this backwards: I'm a lifter who added running, so the mirror version, [adding running to a strength program](/blog/add-running-to-strength-program), is its own article. But I've coached enough runners through their first barbell block to know where they get stuck, and it's almost never the lifting itself. It's fitting two strength sessions around the runs that actually matter without arriving at every workout tired.

Here's how to start.

## What to lift, and how much

You need four movement patterns, not fourteen exercises. Squat, hinge, push, pull. Everything a runner needs from the gym lives in those four, and a beginner gets almost all of the benefit from the compound versions.

A session is:

- **Squat** — back squat or goblet squat, 3 sets of 5.
- **Hinge** — Romanian deadlift or trap-bar deadlift, 3 sets of 6.
- **Push** — overhead press or dumbbell press, 3 sets of 8.
- **Pull** — chin-ups (assisted if needed) or rows, 3 sets of 8.
- **Calf raises** — 2 sets of 12. Boring, and the one accessory that pays for itself in shin and Achilles resilience.

That's it. Five exercises, about 40 minutes. If you have a second session in the week, run the same five — a beginner improves fastest by repeating the lifts and adding weight, not by chasing variety.

**How heavy?** This is where runners either stay too light forever or hurt themselves in week two. Use RPE — how many reps you had left. Every working set should end at RPE 7: three reps left in the tank. Not a grind, not a warm-up. If 5 reps at RPE 7 felt like you could have done 8, add weight next session. If it felt like a true 5, hold. You'll add weight almost every week for the first two months, then it slows down. That slowdown is normal — it's the point where the strength is starting to stick.

Skip failure entirely. A runner has no reason to ever miss a rep in the gym. The last grinding rep costs three days of heavy legs and buys almost nothing.

## How many days, and where they go

Two sessions a week is the answer for almost everyone. One maintains a little; three starts eating into the running for most people who aren't lifting as their main sport. Two is enough to get genuinely stronger and stay there.

The hard part is placement. Your running has a hierarchy — usually one long run and one quality session (intervals or tempo) that matter, and some easy miles that don't. The rule is simple: **protect the runs that matter, put the lifting on the days that don't.**

That means lifting on the same day as an easy run or a hard run, but never the day before your key sessions. A concrete week for someone running four days and lifting twice:

| Day | Session |
|-----|---------|
| Mon | Easy run + strength (lift after the run, or later in the day) |
| Tue | Quality run — intervals or tempo |
| Wed | Rest or easy run |
| Thu | Easy run + strength |
| Fri | Rest |
| Sat | Long run |
| Sun | Rest or easy run |

Notice the lifts sit on Monday and Thursday — 48 hours before Tuesday's quality run and away from Saturday's long run. Legs get to be tired on the easy days, fresh on the days that count. If you can only lift once, make it the day after your long run, when the week's key work is already done.

Lift after you run, not before, when they share a day. Running on legs pre-fatigued from squats is where form falls apart and easy pace drifts slow. The reverse — lifting on slightly tired legs — costs you a little on the bar and nothing that matters. This is the same logic behind [running and lifting on the same day](/blog/running-and-lifting-same-day), which goes deeper on order and spacing.

## Won't this slow my running down?

Not at the doses above. The interference effect — the idea that endurance work blunts strength gains, and the reverse — is real but small, and it bites hardest at high volumes of both. A runner doing two moderate lifts a week is nowhere near that line. If anything the causation runs the other way for you: stronger legs are more economical legs, and the research on how the two directions interact is covered in [does running kill your gains](/blog/interference-effect-does-running-kill-gains).

What will slow your running is doing leg day like a bodybuilder the day before a hard session. Heavy squats and hard running argue over the same tissue for about 48 hours — [leg day and running in the same week](/blog/leg-day-and-running-same-week) is the whole story. Keep the RPE capped at 7, keep failure out of it, and the lifting sits underneath the running instead of on top of it.

The other thing that helps: don't add lifting and add mileage in the same month. Pick one new stress at a time. If you're starting the gym, hold your running volume flat for the first four to six weeks while your body learns the lifts. Your tendons adapt slower than your muscles — the ache in a new movement is on a longer clock than the strength.

## How to progress it

**Weight:** add the smallest jump available (2.5 kg on the bar, one dumbbell size) whenever every set of a lift hit RPE 7 or easier last week. When a set climbs to RPE 8-9, hold the weight until it settles back. The bar goes up fast at first, then a couple of times a month. That's not failure; that's a beginner becoming an intermediate.

**Volume:** leave it alone. Three sets is plenty for a year. Runners don't need more sets; they need consistency and slightly more weight over time.

**When it stalls:** every 8-12 weeks, change the rep scheme — triples on the squat and hinge, keep eights on the upper body — and take an easy week where you drop to 2 sets. That easy week matters more once you're running and lifting; the fatigue from both stacks, and backing both off at once is the fix. [Deload and recovery in a hybrid week](/blog/deload-and-recovery-hybrid-week) covers when to pull the trigger.

## FAQ

### How many strength sessions a week does a runner actually need?

Two. One maintains a little strength; three starts competing with your running unless the gym is your main sport. Two moderate sessions build real strength and are easy to recover from around four or five runs.

### Should I lift before or after my run?

After, when they share a day. Running on legs already fatigued by squats ruins your form and slows your easy pace. Lifting on slightly tired legs costs a little on the bar and nothing that matters to your running.

### Will lifting make me heavier and slower?

Not at two sessions a week of compound lifts without training to failure. You'll gain strength and very little size — beginners get stronger mostly through the nervous system before muscle grows. Stronger, more economical legs make you a faster runner, not a heavier one.
