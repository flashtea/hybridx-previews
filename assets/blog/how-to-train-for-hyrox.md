Hyrox looks like a strength event. Sleds, wall balls, farmers carries, a sandbag on your shoulders. Then you look at the format: eight 1km runs with a station between each. That's over 8 kilometers of running — more like 9 once you count the transition zone — wrapped around strength work. For most first-timers it's well over an hour of continuous effort.

Which means Hyrox is not a strength event that punishes weak runners. It's an endurance race that punishes people who trained strength and running as two separate programs.

That's the trap. Most people preparing for their first Hyrox run their runs from one plan and lift from another — or worse, do CrossFit-style conditioning three times a week and hope the running sorts itself out. Then race day teaches them the lesson every station is designed to teach: the run after the sled push is not the same sport as the run you trained.

## What the race actually asks of you

The format, for every division: 1km run, then a station, eight times over.

| Station | Work | What it actually tests |
|---------|------|------------------------|
| SkiErg | 1000m | Upper-body endurance, pacing discipline |
| Sled Push | 50m | Leg drive — and it wrecks the next run |
| Sled Pull | 50m | Posterior chain, grip |
| Burpee Broad Jumps | 80m | Full-body work capacity, will to live |
| Rowing | 1000m | Aerobic engine, back on tired legs |
| Farmers Carry | 200m | Grip, trunk, walking economy |
| Sandbag Lunges | 100m | Single-leg strength endurance |
| Wall Balls | 75–100 reps | Legs + shoulders when both are gone |

Nothing on that list requires a big one-rep max. A 100kg squatter has all the absolute strength the sled needs. What the race requires is producing moderate force *over and over* while your heart rate is already high from running — and then running again.

Coaches call this **compromised running**: your 1km pace after a 50m sled push is not your fresh 1km pace, and the gap between the two is where Hyrox races are won and lost. You can be a 40-minute 10k runner and still blow up in round three because you never trained the transition.

## The mistake: two programs that don't talk to each other

I spent years as a powerlifter (300kg deadlift) and then learned endurance the hard way, ending up with a 3:28 marathon. The single biggest lesson from that transition applies directly to Hyrox: **strength and endurance training interfere with each other, and the interference is manageable — but only if one plan sees both.**

Concretely, for Hyrox prep:

- Heavy lower-body strength work and quality running compete for the same recovery. Squat hard on Monday and Tuesday's interval session is compromised — fine if it's planned, a slow-motion overuse injury if it's an accident.
- The classic response — "I'll just do everything hard" — caps both adaptations and is the fastest route to showing up on race day tired instead of fit.
- The opposite response — dropping strength work to protect the running — hands back the exact quality the stations test.

If your running plan doesn't know you pushed a sled yesterday, and your lifting app doesn't care about tomorrow's tempo run, *you* are the integration layer. At 9pm, tired, guessing.

## The three pillars of Hyrox training

Everything I've seen work — in the research on concurrent training and in practice — reduces to three things, in this order of importance.

### 1. Easy running volume (the unglamorous 60%)

Hyrox is mostly an aerobic race. The single highest-return block of training is the one nobody posts about: easy runs, week after week. If you're coming from a lifting background, this is your limiter, and no amount of sled work substitutes for it.

Easy means easy — conversational pace, most of your weekly kilometers. The same rule that governs marathon training governs this: **easy runs must be easy**, so the hard sessions can actually be hard.

### 2. Strength endurance, not max strength

The stations reward the ability to repeat submaximal efforts. Train the movements in that rep range:

- Sled pushes and pulls if you have access — heavy carries, hill sprints, or leg-press intervals if you don't
- Lunges, step-ups, and wall balls in sets of 15–25, not 5
- Farmers carries for distance, not load PRs
- Keep one heavy compound (squat or deadlift pattern) in the week to hold your strength base, at maintenance volume — 2–3 working sets is enough

### 3. Compromised running (the Hyrox-specific 10%)

Once a week, put running and station work in the same session and practice the transition itself:

- **Run–station intervals**: 800m–1km run at target race pace → 15–20 wall balls or 20m sled push → repeat 4–6 rounds
- **Race simulation** (every 3–4 weeks): half the race format at target pace, learning what your legs do on the run after lunges
- The goal is calibration, not destruction — you're teaching your body what race pace feels like *after* a station, so on race day nothing is a surprise

## A sample week, 8 weeks out

For someone training 5 days a week with a decent base:

| Day | Session |
|-----|---------|
| Mon | Strength: squat focus + lunges, carries (60 min) |
| Tue | Easy run 8–10km, conversational |
| Wed | Hybrid: 5 rounds of 800m run + station work (wall balls, sled or substitute) |
| Thu | Rest, or 30 min easy spin/row |
| Fri | Strength endurance: pull focus + SkiErg or row intervals |
| Sat | Long easy run 12–16km |
| Sun | Rest |

Two things to notice. The week has more easy aerobic work than station work — that's deliberate. And the hard sessions are spaced so they don't poison each other: the Wednesday hybrid session lands between the strength days, not after leg day.

Closer to race day, the long run holds, the strength volume drops, and the hybrid sessions drift toward full race simulation. The last ten days are a taper — reduce volume, keep intensity, arrive fresh. (The taper logic is the same as [a marathon taper](/blog/marathon-taper-without-losing-strength), just shorter.)

## The mistakes I see most

1. **Training Hyrox like CrossFit.** Random hard conditioning builds fatigue tolerance but not the aerobic base an 8km+ race runs on. The race is too long to muscle through.
2. **Skipping the boring runs.** The physiology doesn't negotiate. If most of your training is stations, your race is decided by round four.
3. **Testing the race in training every week.** Full simulations are expensive. Once every few weeks, done at target pace, teaches you more than weekly destruction.
4. **Letting the two halves collide.** Leg day the day before your quality run, twice a week, is how prep quietly falls apart. The order and spacing of sessions matters as much as their content.
5. **No pacing plan.** The first sled push feels easy on adrenaline. Redline there and rounds five through eight become a death march. Even pacing wins this race for almost everyone below the elite field.
