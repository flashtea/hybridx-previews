The question I get from lifters isn't whether they can run. It's whether running will cost them the squat and deadlift they spent years building. It will cost a little — I wrote the honest ledger of what I lost going from a 300kg deadlift to a marathon in [can you powerlift and run](/blog/can-you-powerlift-and-run), and the short version is 10-15% off the deadlift, more off the squat, almost nothing off the bench. But most of that loss comes from adding running badly: too much, too fast, too hard, in the wrong place in the week. Do it well and the bill is far smaller than gym culture promises.

This is the how-to. Not whether — how. Where the runs go, how far you start, and the one rule that decides everything. If you're coming the other way, a runner adding your first barbell work, start with [a beginner strength plan for runners](/blog/strength-training-for-runners-beginner) instead.

## Start smaller than feels worth it

The first mistake every strong person makes is starting running the way they'd start a new lift: with intent. Three runs in week one, pushing the pace, treating each one as a session to be conquered. Your heart and lungs can handle that in week one. Your shins, feet, and Achilles cannot — they're loaded differently than they've ever been, and they adapt on a scale of months, not weeks.

Start with **two easy runs a week, 20-30 minutes each.** That's it. It will feel like nothing. Do it anyway. The point of the first month isn't fitness — it's letting connective tissue that's never absorbed repeated impact learn to absorb it without complaining. A lifter who starts at two short easy runs and holds there for three weeks almost never gets the shin splints that end most lifters' running before it starts.

**The easy-run rule is the whole game: easy runs must actually be easy.** Conversational. Full sentences, not gasped words. If you track heart rate it's roughly 60-70% of max, but the talk test is more honest than the watch. Coming from lifting — where a set that doesn't feel hard feels wasted — this pace will feel like cheating. It isn't. Easy running is the one kind that doesn't fight your lifts, because it barely touches the recovery your squats and deadlifts need. Hard running does. That's why [zone 2 running for lifters](/blog/zone-2-running-for-lifters) is the running I'd start any strength athlete on and keep them on for months.

## Where the runs go in the week

If you lift four days you already have the hard part half-solved — you have days off, and easy runs go on them. The rule for placement is about protecting your lifts, in this order:

1. **Never put a run the day before your heaviest lower-body day.** Tired legs turn a top-set squat into a grind, and a grind at heavy weight is where you get hurt.
2. **Never put a hard run the day after deadlifts.** Deadlift fatigue sits in exactly the posterior chain a hard run needs and won't get. This one I learned the expensive way.
3. **Easy runs can go almost anywhere** — they're low enough stress to sit the day after a lift or the morning of an upper-body day.

A concrete week for a four-day strength program with running added:

| Day | Session |
|-----|---------|
| Mon | Squat (heavy) + upper accessories |
| Tue | Easy run, 30 min |
| Wed | Bench + upper |
| Thu | Deadlift (heavy) + posterior chain |
| Fri | Easy run, 30 min |
| Sat | Longer easy run, 40-50 min (optional third run) |
| Sun | Rest |

The runs sit away from the heavy days, the long run has nothing heavy in front of or behind it, and no run is asked to happen on freshly deadlifted legs. The lifting is untouched. That's the goal for the first few months: running gets added into the gaps, not on top of the lifts. The general logic of spacing heavy lifts and runs is in [running and lifting on the same day](/blog/running-and-lifting-same-day) and, for the squat specifically, [leg day and running in the same week](/blog/leg-day-and-running-same-week).

## Ramp at 10%, and keep the lifts the priority

Once you've held two or three easy runs for three or four weeks without new aches, you build — slowly. **Add no more than about 10% to your weekly running time each week,** and put the increase into duration, not speed. 60 total minutes becomes 66, not 80. This is the single number that separates lifters who keep running from lifters who get hurt and quit in month two. Your cardiovascular system will beg you to go faster. Your tendons set the pace, and they're slower.

Every fourth week, cut the running back by about a third. This isn't optional recovery — it's the week your connective tissue consolidates what the last three built. Skip it and the aches arrive right when you'd started to trust your feet.

Through all of this, **the lifts stay the priority.** If a week gets tight and something has to give, the run gets cut, not the squat — you're a strength athlete adding running, not a runner now. Keep the big lifts where they were: heavy, low-rep, the same top sets. What you'll trim isn't the main lifts but the accessory volume — the third and fourth sets of leg work that were quietly costing recovery you now need for running. Two to three hard sets of a main lift holds strength nearly as well as five, at a fraction of the recovery cost, and the [interference effect](/blog/interference-effect-does-running-kill-gains) hits the squat hardest precisely because it shares the most tissue with running. Trim there first.

Expect the squat to feel heavier before your body settles — accumulated fatigue shows up around weeks 3-6, the same window where the running finally starts to click. That's the frustrating phase, and it passes. The strength loss levels off; the running keeps improving.

## How much running is "enough" without wrecking strength

For a strength athlete who wants the health and conditioning of running without giving up the platform, **15-25 km a week across two or three easy runs is the sweet spot.** That's enough to drop resting heart rate, improve recovery between heavy sets, and get you comfortably through a 5k or 10k, while costing your lifts almost nothing. It's roughly where I'd cap it if the barbell is still your identity.

Past about 30 km a week, and especially once you add a second hard running session, the interference gets real and the squat starts paying for it. That's a fine trade if your goal has shifted toward a race — but then you're programming as a hybrid athlete, and the week changes shape. How far you can push the day count before something breaks is in [how many days a week should a hybrid athlete train](/blog/how-many-days-a-week-hybrid-athlete). And if the running you're adding is for a job that also tests a timed run or ruck, the sequencing matters even more — [training for a fitness test that scores strength and a run](/blog/tactical-fitness-test-strength-and-run) covers that case.

## FAQ

### How much running can I add before it hurts my squat and deadlift?

Up to about 15-25 km a week of easy running costs a strong lifter very little — often nothing on the deadlift and bench, a small amount on the squat. Past 30 km, or once you add a second hard running session, the interference gets real and strength starts to slide.

### Should I run on my lifting days or rest days?

Easy runs can go on either — they're low stress. The rules that matter: no run the day before a heavy squat, and no hard run the day after deadlifts. Put easy runs on rest days or after an upper-body session, and keep them away from your heaviest lower-body days.

### How fast should my easy runs be?

Slow enough to hold a full conversation — roughly 60-70% of max heart rate. It will feel too easy. That's correct. Easy running is the only running that doesn't compete with your lifts for recovery; running it hard is what costs you the squat.
